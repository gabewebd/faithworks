AI Is Not Another Niche Skill. It Is Becoming a Foundational One.

AI is becoming part of everyday professional work. The opportunity is not to replace expertise, but to combine existing skills with better tools, workflows, and judgment.

For a long time, “AI skills” sounded like something for engineers and data teams. That is changing quickly. AI assistants now sit inside the software most professionals already use: email, documents, spreadsheets, chat and search. You no longer need to build anything to use them. You need to know how to work with them.

That makes AI less like a specialty and more like a baseline skill, in the same way spreadsheets, email and web search once were. Nobody lists “can use email” on a CV any more, but everyone is expected to. AI is heading the same way. This article looks at the evidence, what a working level of AI literacy actually involves, what it changes about a normal working day, and a simple 30-day plan to build it.

The shift is already happening

You don’t have to take this on faith. Two large surveys published in the last two years point in the same direction:

  • Most office workers already use it. Microsoft and LinkedIn’s 2024 Work Trend Index, a survey of 31,000 people in 31 countries, found that 75% of knowledge workers use AI at work. Of those, 78% bring their own AI tools rather than waiting for their employer to provide them.
  • Few have been taught. The same survey found that only 39% of people who use AI at work had received AI training from their company. Most are learning by trial and error.
  • Employers expect it to keep growing. The World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on more than 1,000 employers, lists AI and big data as the fastest-growing skill to 2030, followed by networks and cybersecurity and technological literacy. Employers expect 39% of workers’ core skills to change over the same period.

Put together, the picture is clear. AI use is already widespread, the habits around it are mostly self-taught, and the gap between people who use it well and people who use it badly, or not at all, is widening.

How a specialist skill becomes a basic one

Spreadsheets are a useful comparison. At first they belonged to accountants and analysts. Then they became the default tool for budgets, schedules, inventories and sign-up lists. Most people never learned advanced formulas, but almost everyone learned enough to be useful. The skill didn’t disappear; it spread.

Email followed the same path, and so did web search. Each time, the people who adapted early didn’t become technologists. They simply stopped seeing the tool as someone else’s job, and started using it for their own.

“Foundational” doesn’t mean everyone becomes an AI expert. It means everyone needs a working level of literacy: enough to use the tools on their own tasks, recognize a weak output, and know when not to use them at all.

What AI literacy actually looks like at work

In practice, working AI literacy comes down to five habits. None of them are technical, and all of them can be practised on tasks you already do.

  • Choosing the right tasks. Drafting, summarizing, restructuring and brainstorming suit AI well. Final decisions, sensitive conversations and anything you can’t check do not.
  • Writing a clear brief. Say who the output is for, what it should do, how long it should be and what good looks like. A vague request gets a vague answer.
  • Giving context. Paste in the notes, the previous email, the outline. The tool only knows what you give it.
  • Checking the output. Read it the way a careful editor would: facts, numbers, names, dates, tone. AI can be confidently wrong.
  • Protecting information. Know what your organization allows you to share with which tools, and never paste in data you wouldn’t want stored elsewhere.

An example: the weekly update

Take a typical weekly team update. Done by hand, it means scrolling through chats and notes, then writing from scratch. Done with AI, you paste in your rough notes, say exactly what you want back, and check every item against what actually happened.

The result took seconds and is genuinely useful. But look closely and you will see why the checking step stays yours. “Vendor call moved to Tuesday” has landed under In progress, although it is really a scheduling note. “About half done” has become “approximately 50% complete”, which is a slightly different claim. Neither is a disaster. Both are the kind of small drift that, over a few weeks, makes a manager trust the updates less.

The writing gets faster. The judgment about what matters, and whether it is accurate, is still yours.

AI is starting to look less like a specialty and more like basic literacy.

What doesn’t change: your expertise

AI can draft, summarize, sort and suggest, but it doesn’t know your clients, your market, your team or your standards. The people who get the most from it bring that knowledge to every output. They know what good looks like, so they can direct the tool and catch its mistakes.

That’s why experience becomes more valuable, not less. A junior colleague and a senior one can use the same tool. The senior one usually gets a better result, because they ask better questions and recognize a weak answer faster.

What does change: the shape of the working day

Work that used to take an afternoon can shrink to a fraction of the time. That leaves more room for the parts of the job that need a person: conversations, decisions, relationships and careful thinking. It also changes what “done” means. A first draft arrives sooner, so more of the work moves into reviewing, editing and deciding.

Reviewing, editing and deciding are skills worth practising deliberately. They are what turns a fast draft into good work.

What it looks like in different roles

Because the habits are the same everywhere, AI literacy travels across jobs. A few everyday starting points:

  • Marketing: first drafts of campaign copy and social posts, then a human edit for voice and claims.
  • HR and people: plain-language summaries of policies, and interview question banks to adapt.
  • Finance: explaining variances in a report, or drafting the commentary, with every figure checked against the source.
  • Operations: turning a messy process into clear step-by-step instructions.
  • Education and training: lesson outlines, examples at different levels, practice questions.
  • Sales: preparing for a call by summarizing what you already know about the client and the questions to ask.

Where people usually get stuck

  • Waiting for the “right” tool. Any mainstream assistant is enough to learn the habits. They transfer from one tool to the next.
  • Treating AI like a search box. One short question, one answer, done. It works better as a conversation you refine.
  • Skipping context. Then deciding the tool “isn’t good enough” when it guesses wrong.
  • Trusting the first output. Then losing confidence after one mistake, instead of building a habit of checking.
  • Keeping it private. Many people use AI quietly and never compare notes, so good prompts and lessons stay with one person.

A 30-day plan to build the baseline

You don’t need a course to start. Twenty minutes, two or three times a week, is enough if each session uses a real task.

  1. Week 1: summaries. Long emails, reports and meeting notes. Practise asking for a specific format, such as decisions and owners.
  2. Week 2: first drafts. Routine emails and short documents. Practise giving the audience, purpose and length.
  3. Week 3: thinking partner. Ask it to find gaps in a plan, list risks, or argue the other side. Practise judging which points are useful.
  4. Week 4: review and keep. Look back at what worked. Save your three best prompts in one document and share one with a colleague.

For a simple way to keep this going after the first month, see A Learning Rhythm That Fits Real Life.

Where to start this week

  • Pick one task you repeat every week and do it with AI assistance, start to finish.
  • Write your request like a brief: audience, purpose, length, format.
  • Keep the prompts that work in one document, and reuse them.
  • Review every output before it goes anywhere. That step is yours.

The real shift

The professionals who do well over the next few years won’t necessarily be the most technical. They’ll be the ones who combine what they already know with tools that remove the busywork, and who keep their judgment switched on while they do it. That’s a skill anyone can build, one task at a time.
